A PERSONAL READING · TWELVE BOOKS

Why How to Train Your Dragon Means So Much to Me

When people hear How to Train Your Dragon, many of them probably think of the movies first. The original book series by Cressida Cowell, however, is very different. It begins as a funny children’s story filled with strange Vikings, ridiculous adventures, and silly drawings. But as the twelve books continue, the story slowly grows into something much larger: a story about war, leadership, loss, growing up, and what it really means to be a hero.

“There were dragons when I was a boy.”

A story about growing up, keeping faith, and remembering the world we once had.

It is one of the fantasy series that has affected me the most.

The kind of hero Hiccup chooses to be

What I love most about Hiccup is that he never becomes a hero simply by turning into the kind of person everyone once wanted him to be.

At the beginning, Hiccup is almost the opposite of a traditional Viking hero. He is small, not especially strong, and much more interested in understanding dragons than fighting them. The Viking world around him believes that strength means being louder, tougher, and more violent than everyone else.

At first, Hiccup seems weak because he does not fit this definition.

But as he grows older, something interesting happens.

He actually becomes strong.

He learns to fight. He becomes an excellent swordsman. He gains authority, followers, and eventually the ability to lead others. In other words, he becomes capable of being the traditional hero that everyone once thought he could never be.

And yet, after gaining all that power, he still does not believe that power is the answer to everything.

That is what makes his character so meaningful to me.

Hiccup does not choose peace because he is unable to fight. He does not choose understanding because he is afraid of conflict. By the end of the series, he knows exactly how to fight and what violence can accomplish.

He simply believes there are things that violence cannot solve.

To me, that is much more powerful than the usual story of a weak character becoming strong.

Hiccup’s story is not really:

weak → strong.

It is more like:

a child believes that understanding is stronger than violence → the world tells him he is naive → he grows up and sees how cruel the world can be → he gains real power → and he still chooses to believe what he believed at the beginning.

That is why I think of the series almost as a kind of faith.

It does not tell us that kindness will always be rewarded, or that good people will never lose anything. In fact, Hiccup loses a great deal. The later books become much darker than the early ones. There is war, death, betrayal, and the disappearance of the world he knew as a child.

But the story still refuses to become cynical.

It seems to say that even when hatred has reasons, we do not have to let hatred decide what happens next. Even when violence works, it does not have to become our highest value. Even when we gain power, we can still choose to listen.

I think that is a difficult belief to keep as you grow older.

When the beginning becomes a memory

Another reason the series stays with me is its feeling of time and loss.

The first sentence of the series is:

“There were dragons when I was a boy.”

And at the very end, after everything Hiccup has experienced, the same sentence returns.

When I first read it, it sounded like the opening of a children’s fantasy story.

After twelve books, it means something completely different.

Now it sounds like an old man remembering a world that no longer exists.

That change is heartbreaking because the sentence itself does not change at all. The reader changes.

By the end, Hiccup has spent his life remembering the world he had at the beginning: dragons in the sky, his childhood, his family, his friends, and an age that has already disappeared.

There is another line near the end:

“In my beginning is my end.”

I like to understand it as something close to: my beginning was also my destination.

The world Hiccup would spend the rest of his life remembering was already around him when he was a child.

At the time, he did not know how precious it was.

I think this is one of the saddest and most beautiful ideas in the series.

When we are living through childhood, we do not know that one day it will become “the old world.” We complain about ordinary days, arguments, school, family, and all the little things around us. Only much later may we realize that we were already living inside something we would miss.

Hiccup cannot return to that world.

He can only remember it and tell its story.

Growing strong enough to stay kind

This is also why I think Hiccup gradually becomes more than just a realistic character.

He becomes a symbol.

He represents a certain idea of a hero.

  • A hero is not simply the strongest person in the room.
  • A hero can understand an enemy without agreeing with them.
  • A hero can possess power without worshipping it.
  • A hero can grow older without being ashamed of the kindness he had as a child.
  • Most importantly, growing up does not always mean discovering that your childhood beliefs were foolish.
  • Sometimes growing up means finally becoming strong enough to protect them.

That may be the idea from How to Train Your Dragon that I value most.

Growing up with the books

The series is certainly not perfect. The early books can feel extremely childish, and there are many jokes, exaggerated characters, and ridiculous situations. Someone expecting a serious epic fantasy from the first chapter may be confused.

But I would actually recommend not skipping that part.

The childish beginning is part of what makes the ending so powerful.

You grow up with Hiccup.

The silly Viking world of the early books slowly becomes history. Things that once seemed like jokes become memories. Characters who once seemed simple become part of something much larger.

The story itself seems to grow older with its reader.

That is rare.

What I want to carry with me

There are fantasy books I admire because they are clever, complex, or beautifully written.

How to Train Your Dragon is different.

It is one of the few stories that has become part of the way I think about what kind of person I want to be.

  • It reminds me that becoming stronger does not mean becoming harder.
  • That understanding someone is not the same as surrendering to them.
  • That having the ability to hurt someone does not mean we should.
  • And that growing up does not have to mean losing everything we once believed in.

That is why, for me, How to Train Your Dragon is more than a fantasy series.

It is a story about keeping your original beliefs after you have finally learned how difficult the world really is.

And perhaps that is why, after everything, the story can only end where it began:

“There were dragons when I was a boy.”
