A SIX-BOOK READING JOURNEY · MEGAN WHALEN TURNER

Why I Love The Queen’s Thief

The Queen’s Thief · by Megan Whalen Turner

Across six books, old scenes keep changing meaning. Gen becomes a king with the power to fight, rule, and frighten others; what stays with me is how carefully he chooses the person he will be. This is a story about power, responsibility, and the people who help us remain ourselves.

having power does not mean you have to become the kind of person that power makes possible.

The Queen’s Thief by Megan Whalen Turner is one of my favorite fantasy series, but it is also one of the hardest to explain.

At first, it seems to be about clever tricks, political games, hidden plans, and a thief named Eugenides, or Gen, who is always several steps ahead of everyone else.

But after reading the whole series, I think its real power is somewhere else.

It is not mainly about surprise.

It is about understanding people differently after you learn more about them.

That is what makes the series so memorable to me.

01

THE READER

Why I recommend this series

I would recommend The Queen’s Thief especially to students who like books that become better after you finish them.

The series does not explain everything immediately.

Sometimes an important emotion is hidden behind a joke.

Sometimes a relationship only becomes clear several books later.

Sometimes a sentence that seemed ordinary in Book One becomes painful after Book Six.

That makes the story feel unusually alive.

The characters seem to have histories that existed before the reader understood them.

And the more you understand them, the less simple they become.

For me, the deepest idea in the series is not that intelligence defeats strength.

It is something more difficult:

having power does not mean you have to become the kind of person that power makes possible.
KILLGen can kill.
MANIPULATEHe can manipulate.
RULEHe can rule.
FRIGHTENHe can frighten people.

But the real question is always:

Will he let those abilities decide who he is?

That is why I find him so memorable.

Some fantasy heroes inspire me because they represent an ideal.

Gen affects me differently.

He reminds me that being a good person may not mean being naturally pure, harmless, or certain.

Sometimes it means knowing exactly what you are capable of—

and choosing, again and again, not to become your worst possibility.

That is what makes The Queen’s Thief one of the fantasy series I value most.

02

MEANING ON REREADING

The twists are emotional, not just plot twists

Many fantasy books have twists that make you say:

“I did not know that happened.”

The Queen’s Thief often makes me say:

“I did not know that was what it meant.”

The facts of the past usually do not change.

Instead, a later book gives you one new piece of information, and suddenly an old scene feels completely different.

A character who once seemed cold may have been protecting someone.

A joke may have been hiding fear.

A cruel decision may have come from love, duty, or desperation.

Nothing is erased. Nothing is replaced.

The meaning simply becomes deeper.

That is why this series is so rewarding to reread.

The first time, I often wanted to know:

What is Gen planning?

The second time, I started asking:

Who knows what? Who is protecting whom? What is someone refusing to say?

That change is one of the things I love most about the series.

03

POWER & CHOICE

Gen is powerful, but he is afraid of what power can make him

Gen is not interesting because he is weak and learns to become strong.

He is already dangerous.

He is intelligent, manipulative, politically talented, and very skilled with a sword. Even as a child, he had already learned that he was capable of killing.

That is why his attitude toward violence matters so much.

Early in the series, Gen says that he does not take up a sword unless his life is in danger.

At first, this can sound like another part of his rebellious personality. His father is a soldier and wants Gen to become one too, while Gen insists on being a Thief instead.

But after the final book reveals more about his childhood, this rule feels much heavier.

Gen is not avoiding swords because he is unable to fight.

He is avoiding them because he knows he can.

That difference completely changes the way I see him.

His mercy is not innocence.
It is restraint.

He knows there is a version of himself that could become cruel, feared, and completely alone.

The series never pretends that this possibility is impossible.

That is what makes his choices meaningful.

04

FAMILY & DUTY

Gen and his father

The relationship between Gen and his father is one of my favorite parts of the series because it is so quiet.

His father wants him to become a soldier.

Gen spends much of his youth rejecting that identity.

Their conflict seems simple at first:

Father: become a warrior.

Gen: I am a Thief.

But the later books make their relationship much more complicated.

We learn that his father has protected Gen in ways Gen did not always understand. At one point, even sending him into danger was partly a way of keeping him alive.

That is the kind of twist Turner does best.

The past does not change.

Gen’s father was still strict.

They still argued.

Gen still felt misunderstood.

But suddenly you realize:

his father may have loved him much more actively than Gen understood at the time.

He simply expressed love through responsibility, protection, training, and action rather than words.

Their confrontation during the king’s trial in the final book is especially powerful to me.

Gen, who spent his youth refusing to become the warrior his father wanted, now has to stand against his father with a sword.

By this point, Gen has become a king and a military leader.

But the meaning is not:

“His father was right all along.”

It is almost the opposite.

Gen has learned to carry the responsibilities of a warrior without becoming his father.

And his father has begun to understand that perhaps Gen was right to resist this life.

They finally understand each other more clearly, but only after years of conflict.

That is much more moving to me than a simple reconciliation.

05

THE SWORD

The meaning of the sword changes

The sword is almost a symbol of Gen’s whole character arc.

When Gen is young, the sword represents an identity being forced on him.

It means:

soldier, violence, duty, his father’s expectations.

So he refuses it.

Later, after losing his hand, he has to relearn how to fight. Eventually he proves that he is still an extraordinary swordsman.

But this does not mean he suddenly begins to love violence.

Instead, the meaning of the sword changes.

At first:

I refuse the sword because I refuse to become what others want me to be.

Later:

I will use the sword because I have chosen to accept responsibility for other people.

That difference is very important.

Gen’s growth is not:

idealistic teenager → realistic adult who accepts violence.

It is more like:

freedom → responsibility.

He still dislikes what violence does.

He still prefers tricks, plans, theft, and cleverness.

But eventually, being a king means he cannot always walk away from the cost.

To me, that is a very mature kind of character development.

06

LOVE & MUTUALITY

Irene does not simply need Gen to save her

Another reason I love the series is the relationship between Gen and Irene.

At first, it is easy to think of their story as:

Gen saves Irene from becoming a completely cold and ruthless ruler.

And that is partly true.

But later, Eddis says something even more important:

Irene saved Gen too.

That changes their relationship for me.

Gen could have become another kind of terrifying person: someone brilliant, powerful, violent, and completely alone.

Irene did not “save” him by making him gentle or harmless.

She saved him by giving him a relationship strong enough to stop power, anger, and isolation from becoming his whole identity.

And Gen does something similar for her.

So their relationship is not:

one good person saving one damaged person.

It is two people stopping each other from becoming the worst versions of themselves.

I find that much more interesting.

07

LOSS & BECOMING

Gen feels tragic because his choices always have a cost

This is one major difference between Gen and many fantasy heroes.

He wins, but winning does not erase damage.

His hand does not grow back.

The people he loses do not return.

The violence of his childhood does not disappear.

His father’s death is not made meaningless just because Gen survives.

And he can never fully return to being the young Thief who could escape through a window and leave responsibility behind.

That is why Gen feels tragic to me.

He is not a symbol of a perfect hero.

He is a person who has been permanently changed by what he has survived.

His story asks:

How do you stay yourself when power, pain, love, and responsibility keep changing you?

And I think the answer the series gives is not simple.

Sometimes you cannot stay exactly the same.

Sometimes maturity means accepting things you once rejected.

But you can still decide what kind of person you will become.
